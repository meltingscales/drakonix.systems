                     OUR SOUTHERN FRIEND
  TWO rosy-cheeked children stand at the top of a long hill as the glow of the
winter sunset lights up the snow covered country-side. ”It’s time to go home“
says my sister. She is the eldest. After one more exhilarating trip on the sled,
we plod homeward through the deep snow. The light from an oil lamp shines

      from an upstairs window of our home. We stamp the snow from our boots and
      rush in to the warmth of the coal stove which is supposed to heat up-stairs as
      well. ”‘Hello dearies,“ calls Mother from above, ”get your wet things off.“
        ”Where’s Father?“ I ask, having gotten a whiff of sausage cooking through
      the kitchen door and thinking of supper.
        ”He went down to the swamp,“ replies Mother. ”He should be home soon.“
        Father is an Episcopal minister and his work takes him over long drives on
      bad roads. His parishioners are limited in number, but his friends are many, for
      to him race, creed, or social position make no difference. It is not long before
      he drives up in the old buggy. Both he and old Maud are glad to get home. The
      drive was long and cold but he was thankful for the hot bricks which some

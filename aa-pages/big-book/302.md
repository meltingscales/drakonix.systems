                                RIDING THE RODS
         FOURTEEN years old and strong, I was ready—an American Whittington
      who knew a better way to get places than by walking. The ”clear the way“
      whistle of a fast freight thundering over the crossing on the tracks a mile away
      was a siren call. Sneaking away from my farm home one night, I made my way
      to the distant yards. Ducking along a lane between two made-up trains that
      seemed endless, I made my way to the edge of the yards. Here and there I
      passed a silent, waiting figure. Then a little group talking among themselves.
      Edging in, I listened eagerly. I had met my first hoboes. They talked of places
      I had never heard of. This town was good. A fellow could get by on the Bowery
      all winter if he knew the ropes; that other town was ”hostile“; thirty days for
      ”vag“ awaited you in another if you didn’t hit the cinders before the road ”bulls“
      fine-combed the train.
         Then they noticed me. Somehow a new kid is always an object of interest to
      the adventurers of the rails. ”Where ye makin’ for, Kid?“
         I had heard one of them mention ”Detroit“ and it seemed as good an answer
      as any. I had no plans, just wanted to get away—anywhere—just away!
